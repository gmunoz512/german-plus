
### the tabs

growing up, i always felt like i had 100 tabs open at once. when i was younger it was fun. double-click into whichever one caught my attention and see where it took me.

but as i got older that got harder. i spent more time using ctrl+f to find what i needed instead of just connecting the dots. if you never lost that touch, imo, you're badass.

with age came education, self-diagnosis, and learning to work with my brain instead of against it. quick note: i'm not a doctor and this isn't medical advice. it's just me figuring out how my own head works and what helps.

### what's actually going on when you read

here's the part that nerded me out. your eyes don't glide across a line of text. they hop.

each stop is called a **fixation**, and it lasts about a quarter of a second (roughly 200–250ms). each hop between stops is a **saccade**, usually about 7–9 letters long. you only take in information during the stops. during the hops you're basically blind, and your brain covers it up so smoothly you never notice.

it gets weirder. while you're parked on one word, you're already getting a blurry preview of the next one off to the side (researchers call it *parafoveal preview*). short, obvious words like "the" or "and" get skipped a lot of the time. and the first few letters of a word carry a lot of weight in how fast you recognize it.

so reading isn't really reading every letter. it's guessing. your brain is a prediction machine, constantly betting on what comes next and only checking the evidence when it has to. that's the jumping jacks i was talking about. some of us are just doing them faster and messier.

### attention, but make it interest-based

the way i've come to understand my brain: it doesn't run on "important." it runs on "interesting." people in the adhd world call this an interest-based nervous system. that's a popular framing, not a lab finding, but it matches my life a little too well.

the flip side is **hyperfocus**. when something clicks, it completely consumes me. researchers have actually started taking this seriously as its own state of attention, not just a meme. the hard part is that same brain loses its place in a paragraph three times in a row when the topic isn't grabbing it. working memory drops the thread and you're back at the top of the page.

### finding bionic reading

then i found bionic reading. it bolds the first part of each word, like **rea**ding **thi**s. the idea is your eyes catch the bold start, your brain fills in the rest, and you get a little head start on the guess it's already making.

idk how, but i actually remember what i read with it. i even ended up making a little plugin so it overlays whatever site i'm on.

### the honest part

i did the research, so i owe you this. **the science mostly says it doesn't work.**

- readwise ran an informal test in 2022 with about 2,000 people. bionic readers were 2.6 words per minute *slower* on average, which is basically no difference. comprehension was the same.
- in 2024 joshua snell published a peer-reviewed study literally titled "no, bionic reading does not work." 100 paragraphs, no difference in reading time.
- a study of 490 high schoolers found no difference in speed or comprehension. a small eeg study didn't find an extra benefit for adhd readers either.

a few smaller studies found it helped some students' motivation and confidence. and in readwise's data, about half of people *were* faster with it. it's just that the other half were slower by about the same amount.

so here's where i landed. it probably doesn't make anyone a faster reader. what it might do for me is give my eyes an anchor. when my attention drifts, the bold starts make it easier to notice and land back on the line. part of it is probably novelty. part of it is that it *feels* like focus, and feeling focused makes me stay. that's my experience, not a universal fact. and honestly that's fine. glasses don't work for everyone either.

### the dis·ability part

reading was just one tab. the bigger thing i've been chewing on is the word itself. dis-ability. for years i only heard the first half.

here's the balance i try to keep. the "deficit" side is real. russell barkley, one of the biggest names in adhd research, frames it as a problem of self-regulation: holding back the impulse, keeping the plan in working memory, choosing future-you over right-now-you. brain imaging work by nora volkow's team linked adhd to differences in the dopamine reward pathway, tied to lower motivation for stuff that doesn't pay off right away. that's why "boring but important" feels physically heavy. late bills, half-read emails, the whole thing. i'm not going to pretend that's a superpower.

but look closer and the *ability* is sitting right inside the same wiring.

- **divergent thinking.** holly white and priti shah found adults with adhd came up with more unusual ideas than their peers, and later scored higher on originality when inventing new phone features. they did worse at narrowing down to one right answer. same brain, both sides.
- **what people say themselves.** a 2022 study asked 206 adults with adhd what's *good* about it. almost everyone had an answer. creativity, energy, flexibility, enthusiasm.
- **hyperfocus.** in interviews with successful adults with adhd, hyperfocus, divergent thinking and adventurousness came up as strengths specific to adhd. researchers are starting to treat hyperfocus as a real attention state worth studying, not just a meme.
- **building things.** johan wiklund studied entrepreneurs with adhd and found impulsivity pushed them to just *start*, and hyperfocus decided how it turned out, good or bad. both.

that "good or bad" is the honest part. hyperfocus that ships a project at 3am is the same hyperfocus that skips dinner and forgets a friend's birthday.

### wrong room, not wrong brain

there's a fun theory that adhd traits fit a hunter's world better than a farmer's: always scanning, chasing new stuff, moving fast. one study of ariaal tribesmen in kenya found a dopamine gene variant linked to adhd and novelty-seeking went with better nutrition in men who were still nomadic, but worse nutrition in men who had settled down.

big caveat: that's one small study and a lot of speculation. the hunter-farmer story is more metaphor than science. but the metaphor helps me. a lot of what hurts is a mismatch with the room. fluorescent lights, eight hours of the same thing, tasks with no visible payoff. i can't redesign the world, but i can redesign my corner of it.

### realistic goals for curiosity sprints

this is where it gets practical. for those random sprints where something completely consumes my attention, i've learned to set realistic goals so i don't abandon a project cold turkey when my brain finds its next shiny object.

so i scope small enough to finish *inside* the sprint. one working piece, not the whole vision. write down where i stopped, so future-me has a door back in. and when the sprint ends, i let it end without calling it failure. the curiosity is the engine. the goals are just the brakes and steering.

### an ability, not just a disability

psychologically, one of the coolest things i've learned is that we have an ability, not just a disability.

not instead of. *inside* of. dis·**ability**. the hard parts are real, and if they're running your life, talking to an actual professional beats any blog post, this one included. but the 100 tabs aren't only noise. they're why i notice the weird connection, start the thing, and stay up chasing it.

so i'm not trying to close the tabs. i'm learning which ones to keep open.
